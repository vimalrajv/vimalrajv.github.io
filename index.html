The Unspoken Parallels: Why LLM Pre-training and HPC Simulations Speak the Same Language

By Vimalraj V | #HPC #LLM #AI #MachineLearning #DistributedComputing

I have hopped across various HPC industries, from Oil and Gas to Automotive to Engineering Simulations, and today I am deep into the world of scaling and AI. As I made this transition, I kept running into a sense of déjà vu. Not the mystical kind, but the deeply technical kind. The challenges we tackle to speed up LLM training, the inefficiencies we are battling, and the architectural choices hailed as breakthroughs are not really new. They are the same problems HPC engineers have been wrestling with for decades, just under new names and on shinier hardware.

The more I thought about it, the more it became clear that LLM pre-training isn't some radical departure from HPC. It's actually HPC evolution wearing a transformer hat. And honestly? We need to talk about the terminology.

When Terms Feel Like Old Friends

In the AI community, there is a tendency to reframe concepts that have long been established in HPC, sometimes presenting them as new innovations.

"Data parallelism", splitting data into batches across GPUs for that sweet speedup. Straight out of the MPI playbook, where we'd decompose domains and sync boundaries. That's been standard practice in CFD simulations since the 1990s.

"Tensor parallelism", slicing those giant weight matrices. That's what we did when we partitioned finite element (FEM) stiffness matrices across compute nodes in structural mechanics codes. Same principle, different application.

"Pipeline parallelism", HPC has been pipelining computational stages across nodes for decades. For example, staged execution from CFD workflows. The specific implementation for transformers is new, but the concept of breaking sequential work into stages and overlapping execution across different processors is foundational HPC.

"Gradient checkpointing" (or "activation checkpointing"), store less, recompute more during backprop. That's checkpoint/restart in HPC, and we've been doing it since tape drives. There are also mathematical frameworks for deciding optimal checkpoint frequency.

"Gradient accumulation", faking big batches with small ones mirrors CFD domain decomp with lazy syncs. We've been doing this in parallel PDE solvers forever.

"Mixed precision training", HPC numerical analysts have been managing precision hierarchies (float64/float32/float16) for numerical stability since GPUs were invented. The specific application to neural networks is new, but the principle of "use lower precision where you can, higher precision where you must" is classic numerical computing.

The list goes on. AllReduce, Standard MPI collective operation since 1994. Ring-AllReduce, We've been using ring topologies for collective communication in HPC clusters for years.

"Flash Attention's tiling and kernel fusion", That's cache-aware algorithm design and operation fusion—core HPC optimization techniques from the early 2000s.

I'm not saying the ML implementations aren't sophisticated or valuable. They absolutely are. But when I hear someone describe ZeRO optimizer as revolutionary, I think: "We've been partitioning optimizer state and doing state sharding in parallel solvers for decades." The specific application to transformer training is new, but the underlying memory optimization strategy is not.

The Fundamental Problem: Both Are Solving the Same Equation

Whether you're simulating fluid dynamics around a wind turbine or training an LLM with 70 billion parameters, you're facing the same core constraint: the compute-to-communication ratio is broken. Your GPUs (or CPUs) can perform billions of floating-point operations per second, but moving data around the system? That's the real bottleneck.

In HPC, we've known this for years. The Floating Point Rate divided by Memory Bandwidth is what we call the "balance ratio," and it's been steadily worsening as GPUs get faster but interconnects don't. A modern GPU might have enough arithmetic throughput to solve a problem, but if it spends most of its time waiting for data to arrive from memory or from neighboring nodes, you're just spinning your wheels. Your 80% of peak performance becomes 20% of peak performance.

LLM training faces exactly the same physics. When you're training a transformer, you're not just doing computation—you're constantly shuffling massive tensors around. During the prefill phase (when you process the entire input sequence), you're doing matrix-matrix multiplications, which is compute-bound and loves parallelism. During the decode phase (generating tokens one at a time), you're doing matrix-vector multiplications, which are memory-bound and make the GPU sit idle, watching bandwidth.

The solutions claimed cutting-edge in ML—fused kernels, overlapping computation with communication, mixed precision arithmetic—are the exact playbook HPC has been running for 15 years.

The Shared Enemy: The Communication-to-Compute Bottleneck

Both HPC and LLM training are fundamentally limited by the same physics: data motion is expensive, and it's getting more expensive relative to computation.

In fluid dynamics simulations running on Cray supercomputers, the bottleneck is often interconnect bandwidth between nodes. In LLM training on GPU clusters, the bottleneck is often NVLink bandwidth between GPUs or network bandwidth between nodes. The specific hardware is different, but the fundamental constraint is identical.

This is why both domains obsess over the same metrics:

Memory bandwidth efficiency: Can you saturate the available bandwidth, or are you leaving performance on the table?

Communication volume: Can you reduce the amount of data you're moving?

Synchronization overhead: Are workers waiting for each other unnecessarily?

The HPC community solved similar problems by being ruthlessly pragmatic.

We've been doing this for years.

From "Finishes off in style" to AI tone: How AI failed in a failing test match

By Vimalraj V | #Cricket #AI #Sports #Commentary #Technology

I was watching India's cricket against South Africa, a terrible watch, really. South Africa whitewashed a hapless Indian cricket team, the kind of performance that leaves you deflated. But beyond the poor batting and bowling, something else struck me as deeply odd: listening to the test match day's recap narrated by AI. While the match itself felt like a disaster, the AI commentary felt like a different kind of problem entirely, one that made me reconsider what cricket commentary actually means to those of us who watch the game.

The Sound of the Game: More Than Words

Cricket commentary isn't just play-by-play description. It's the emotional and cultural glue that connects us to the sport. A great commentator doesn't narrate cricket; they consecrate it, turning moments into memories that last lifetimes.

These iconic lines "Dhoni finishes off in style", "By the barest of margins", "Remember the name" are not merely clever descriptions; they are emotional anchors. The true measure of great cricket commentary lies not in the words, but in the memory they engrave. The narration doesn't just describe the play; it provides the sonic memory. Every replay triggers that same electric rush, pulling the listener back into the original, timeless glory.

The Prosody Plunge, Where AI lines kill the Drama

When I listened to that AI-generated test match day summary, everything was technically competent. The statistics were accurate. The structure was logical. The narration was clear. And yet, something deeply uncomfortable nagged at me.

The AI presented the day as a sequence: this happened, then that happened, here are the numbers. But a test cricket day isn't a list of events, it's a three-session narrative arc with psychological depth, momentum shifts, the ebbs and flows that unfolds across multiple dimensions. The AI treated it like a data report, not a story. What made me grimace and reach for the remote wasn't just the lack of emotion, it was that the AI was speaking about cricket without actually understanding what cricket is.

The real problem was that I expected to hear a human voice bringing meaning to the chaos. Instead, I got information presented as narration. Information tells you what happened; narration explains why it matters. AI was doing one; I needed the other.

The Prosody Problem

When a human commentator says "That's a brilliant delivery," the intonation carries real meaning, the same words with rising inflection scream appreciation and admiration, while falling tones drip with resignation as the batsman gets beaten. That emotional architecture flows naturally from genuine understanding of the moment.

AI text-to-speech can't touch this. Modern systems shatter language into phonemes with zero semantic depth, pronouncing "brilliant" perfectly but clueless whether the delivery deserves excitement, regret, or nothing. They fake it through pattern-matching, spotting "brilliant" in hyped contexts and cranking up the animation, but it's just algorithmic simulation, not emotion born from comprehension.

That's exactly what I heard: applied, staged, mechanical feeling that hit my ear instantly. There's a world of difference between a voice that believes what it's saying and one running statistical probabilities.

The Future of Expressive AI

I don't think AI will replace human commentary, but I do believe the technology will create entirely new ways for audiences to experience cricket. The question isn't whether AI will become as good as Ravi Shastri, it's what new possibilities emerge when AI and human expertise combine.

Positive Benchmarks: Football as the Testing Ground

Research into AI sports commentary is advancing rapidly, and football is showing us the future. The SportR benchmark demonstrates that state-of-the-art AI systems achieve approximately 71% accuracy on complex sports reasoning tasks. While that might seem modest, it's actually quite good when you consider the difficulty: these systems are learning to understand tactical formations, rule interpretations, and strategic implications and not just describing what's on screen.

IBM's ProsodyLM has shown that adding fine-grained controls for pitch, cadence, emphasis, and pauses can dramatically elevate the naturalness and emotional realism of long-form generated speech, especially for expressive domains like sports commentary. Their research revealed that today's LLM-to-speech systems don't actually lack language, they lack rhythmic contour and vocal dynamics, which is why synthetic commentary often sounds flat. Going forward, ProsodyLM aims to enable real-time prosody-conditioned generation so AI voices can dynamically adjust excitement, tension, and narrative flow, a crucial step toward truly human-like live sports narration.

Last Thought

That feeling I had listening to the AI summary wasn't frustration with incomplete technology. It was accurate perception of what was being delivered: information presented as narration, data organized as storytelling, voice without presence.

Sports commentary will remain fundamentally human for culturally significant matches because commentary is culture and connection. That voice needs to be real.

But AI won't disappear. It's AI and humans figuring out how to work together in ways that expand the game, not diminish what makes it meaningful. The future of cricket commentary isn't AI becoming human. It's AI handling what doesn't require being human.

Until then to the broadcasters making me watch to AI commentary, here's Nasser's legendary comment to a specy in 2019, "You cannot do that Ben Stokes" IYKYK
